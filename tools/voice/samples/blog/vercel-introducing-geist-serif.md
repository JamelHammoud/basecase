# Introducing Geist Serif

30 Oct 2026

Geist started as a monospace typeface for developers, built for the editors and terminals where they spend their day doing their work. From there it grew into Geist Sans for product interfaces, and then into Geist Pixel, which was inspired by the grids of early computer screens.

Today, we're adding a new member to the family: Geist Serif.

Geist Serif is a high-contrast display serif built on the same foundations as Geist Sans, Geist Mono, and Geist Pixel. It has fine, saw-toothed serifs and sharp hairlines, and it was drawn to communicate messages powerfully both in the physical world and on the high-density screens on modern devices.

## Same system, new voice

At Vercel, Geist Sans runs the product. For headlines, though, we wanted a second voice, one that belongs to the same system, instead of a stranger picked from a drop-down menu.

A lot of tech brands have moved away from clean sans-serifs to get some personality back, and many of them borrow from vintage computer ads. Geist Serif takes a different path: toward fashion, editorial design, and magazine headlines. It's our homage to the artists behind Rolling Stone and Harper's Bazaar.

From the beginning, each Geist family has read from a chapter of type history: the Swiss design movement, early terminal and DOS computing, and now editorial print.

## Display first

Geist Serif began as a set of sketches by Evil Rabbit, which Andrés Briganti then expanded. The first letters already showed an expressive serif, in the spirit of typefaces like Victor Serif and Stanley.

When Mark Davis took over the drawings, we made the first release a display face for headlines, posters, and other large sizes, while work on the full family continued. Print has always struggled to hold fine hairlines and sharp serifs, but today's displays can handle a high level of detail. We wanted the new face to take full advantage of modern screens, so we thinned every downstroke by 50% and tightened the sidebearings to push the contrast as far as it would go.

Each serif has a wedge on one side and a curve on the other, so the proportions are classical and look beautiful in print, but the construction is new and optimized for digital devices.

“We created something that could only be made for today. Not overly blocky for screens from a decade ago, not filled with mechanical compromises for physical ink. Just serifs at perfect points. ”

Mark Davisfounder & font designer at WORDMARK

## Geist grew teeth

The goal was to honor our original intent and keep the serif closely tied to the sans, even when the letters sit on top of each other. Geist Serif’s stems match Geist Sans, and the two share widths and postures.

The saw-toothed serifs and high contrast give Geist an editorial voice. One phrase kept coming up as each character was drawn: “Geist grew teeth.”

## Crafted by hand

In July the font had 227 glyphs. It now has 894 and covers 352 Latin-script languages, and we worked through more than 52,000 kerning pairs to get the spacing right. Some of the details are tiny. The eye of the g, for example, is tilted exactly 7°.

## What’s next

Regular is the first style we’re releasing, and the rest of the family is already in progress. We’re testing future versions while building this one, so this release is just the start.

### Weights from Thin to Black

The variable font will range from Thin (100) to Black (900). In this week’s proofs, the g in Black is the letter we can’t stop looking at.

### Toward a text face

To explore a text version, we built a foundry tool that measures strokes, including where they meet, and thickens the display outlines. It edits the outlines directly, so we can set real words and see which strokes need more weight.

### Italics, built rule by rule

We’re also developing an italic generated entirely in Python. We slanted existing italics back upright and compared them with their romans. With the angle removed, the remaining differences show up clearly, and we turned them into rules we can test and revise. This italic won’t be the final version, but breaking glyphs in code helps us find the limits faster.

### More scripts and Google Fonts

Cyrillic support is planned to match Geist Sans and Geist Mono, and Geist Serif will come to Google Fonts after launch.

## One family

With Geist Sans, Geist Mono, Geist Pixel, and now Geist Serif, the family runs from UI text and code to expressive display type, and all four feel at home together.
