# Cameras

Part of the Basecase guide. [AGENTS.md](../../AGENTS.md) holds the rules every change follows and the index of these files; section numbers (§) are shared across them.

## 8l. Cameras

What a robot sees: the camera parts on it, the stationary camera views placed in the scene, their lenses, the live views beside the canvas, the agent seeing and operating through them, the depth eye a policy observes, and the LiDARs that scan in the sim. The code lives in `src/cameras/`, the strip in `src/components/cameras/`, the eye in `src/sim/eyes.ts`.

- **One camera model.** `cameraViews(robot, world, fixed)` (`views.ts`) lists the views every consumer uses: the stationary cameras (or, when there are none, the dataset export's framed **Scene** view), then one per camera part (catalog group Cameras or Depth Cameras), keyed in snake case. Part cameras come from `partCameras` in `src/datasets/cameras.ts` (lens port, or out of the face opposite the back ports) and ride on their body through `simDeltas`. The live strip, the dataset videos, the agent's `simulate` cameras, `operate` and the depth eye all start from it.
- **Lenses** (`lens.ts`): `{ model: 'pinhole', hfovDeg }` (20–120°) or `{ model: 'fisheye', fovDeg }` (120–240°, equidistant: r = f·θ, the image circle spans the frame's width, black outside). `lensDirection` and `lensPoint` map normalized image points (u right, v up, both −1…1) to camera-frame directions (x right, y up, −z forward) and back; the fisheye shader, the frustum glyph and the eye's rays all use them, so they can't drift apart.
  - **From the catalog** (`optics.ts`, `specLens`): the first spec row that names an angle, in the order FOV / Field of view, Depth, Color, Lens, Camera, Stereo. `H° × V°` takes H; a single angle marked diagonal converts to horizontal for 4:3 (pinhole through the tangent, fisheye linearly, ×0.8); horizontal over 120° or diagonal of 140° or more is a fisheye. No angle: 70° pinhole.
  - **Per part:** `ScenePart.cameraLens` overrides the catalog (a wrist camera with a fisheye M12 lens). The tile's Lens menu sets it; Catalog Lens clears it.
- **Stationary cameras** (`fixed.ts`, `project.cameras`, at most 8): `{ id, name, position, target, lens }` in scene mm. They aren't parts: no BOM, Checks or MJCF. Add Camera View (⌘K and the canvas right-click menu) saves the viewport's current framing as `Camera N` with a pinhole lens at the viewport's horizontal field of view. The canvas draws each as a small frustum (`three/viewport/cameraGlyphs.ts`, on `stage.overlays`, so captures never show it). Delete asks first.
- **Live views** (`CameraStrip`): labelled tiles in a column at the canvas's top left whenever the scene has a camera part or a stationary camera, and while a simulation is loaded. A Cameras header with a chevron collapses and expands them (⇧V, or Collapse / Expand Camera Views in ⌘K and the canvas menu, listed only while the strip shows: `hasCameraViews` in `views.ts`); collapsed, nothing renders. Add Camera View lives in ⌘K and the canvas menu, not the strip. A click enlarges a tile to 640 × 480; right-click opens Look Through (moves the viewport there), then for a camera part or stationary camera Rename (the stationary camera's own name, or the camera part's name) and Lens ▸, and for stationary cameras Use Current View and Delete. The framed Scene view has no name or lens of its own to change, so its menu is Look Through alone (`cameraMenu.tsx`). The feed reloads the worker's stage only when the parts, world, model or the views' geometry change, and keeps drawing on the old stage until the new one is ready, so the strip's re-renders during a simulation never stall it. The lens shows on hover as an overlay. The strip's feed, menu and renaming live in `useCameraTiles`, which a running Case (§8h) shares: there its camera panels draw the same tiles as wide as their card from one feed, always drawing, and the canvas shows no strip. The feed also runs while a code panel has a frame stream open (below), and draws tiles only while they show, so a Case whose code never asks for frames costs no worker, and code that asks costs no tiles. World objects a Case's code hides (`visible: false` in `sim.worldParts`) don't reach the feed, so no camera sees them.
  - **Rendering runs in its own worker and never waits on the main thread** (`feed.worker.ts`, `liveStage.ts`, main side `useCameraFeed.ts`, messages in `feedProtocol.ts`):
    - **Poses go straight from the sim worker to the camera worker** over a `MessageChannel` (`Simulation.connectCameras`; the sim worker posts a copy of a frame's poses to the port when a body moved more than `POSE_EPSILON` since the last one it posted, 0.01 mm or 1e-5 of a quaternion (`sim/poseGate.ts`), so a robot at rest costs no camera frames; `sim.worker.ts` `cameras`). The main thread only receives finished frames: one `ImageBitmap` per tile, shown with `transferFromImageBitmap` (no copy).
    - **One persistent stage**: the WebGL renderer, lights, environment map and floor are made once per worker. A scene change rebuilds only the parts whose objects changed (parts are immutable, so identity says what changed), then compiles their shaders with `compileAsync` before they're drawn; a new sim model or new views are their own messages and rebuild nothing. The main side skips any message whose parts, world, model or views are the same as the last one sent, so the strip's re-renders during a simulation cost nothing.
    - **Paced to the display and the GPU, idle when nothing moves**: a draw is scheduled when poses, views or the scene change, at most one per 1/60 s, always with the latest poses (older ones are dropped). `requestAnimationFrame` in workers doesn't fire reliably, so pacing is by timer. When the sim is paused or stopped, tiles don't draw; open frame streams (below) keep their rate.
      - **At most three frames on the GPU** (`three/gpuFence.ts`): each draw ends with a `fenceSync`, and the next waits (polling every 4 ms) while three are unfinished. GL calls return before the GPU draws, so a timer alone queues frames faster than a slow GPU finishes them. In CI's Linux e2e (software GL), a LiDAR rover's Scene view queued that way until the canvas drew no frame for minutes, and Stop Try never became clickable (#474).
    - **Every view renders into one 640 × 480 GL canvas** through its viewport (`LensRenderer.render(…, viewport)`) and is copied on the GPU into that tile's own canvas, so no canvas is ever resized per frame. Tiles are 320 × 240, an enlarged one 640 × 480.
    - `e2e/cameras.e2e.test.ts` drives a rover with a stationary camera and checks the strip's `data-frames` climbs at a realtime rate: frames the GPU took, since the fence holds back the rest. It measures only after the first frame: a fresh feed worker's first draw compiles every shader at once, which takes seconds on CI's software GL (SwiftShader, no `KHR_parallel_shader_compile`).
  - **Fisheye** (`lensRender.ts`, `LensRenderer`): a cube capture at the lens, then a full-screen pass through the equidistant mapping with tone mapping and sRGB applied there. Only the cube faces the lens can see are rendered (a face counts when its axis is within half the field of view plus its 54.7° half-diagonal of the lens axis; for up to 240° that drops at least the face behind), and the face size follows the pixels the lens spends per degree (width × 90 ÷ field of view × 1.15, 128–1024), one cached cube per size. The dataset export draws through the same renderer, so its videos can be fisheye too.
- **The operator camera** (`src/cameras/operator/`, the `camera`, `hand` and `face` inputs, §8h): Drive with Camera, or a Case whose console names one of them, opens the computer's camera at 640 × 480 (`operatorCamera.ts`, a module store) and a person's arms, hand or face drive the robot.
  - **One stream, one worker, several landmarkers.** Each source joins the session by kind (`joinOperatorCamera('body' | 'hand' | 'face', robot)`) and leaves it on close (`leaveOperatorCamera`): the first join opens the stream and the worker, a later one adds its landmarker to the same worker, and the last to leave stops every track and ends the worker. There is never a second `getUserMedia` (§0f). The worker loads only the landmarkers of the kinds that joined and runs each on the latest frame (`runners.ts`), and each join resolves when its own landmarker is ready.
  - **Estimation runs in its own worker** (`pose.worker.ts`, messages in `poseProtocol.ts`, loaders in `landmarkers.ts`): MediaPipe's Pose, Hand (one hand) and Face Landmarkers (`@mediapipe/tasks-vision`, `VIDEO` mode, GPU with a fallback to CPU), with their models in `public/models/` (`pose_landmarker_lite.task`, `hand_landmarker.task`, `face_landmarker.task`, float16). Each landmarker loads its own copy of the wasm, so a session with all three holds three. Their loader and wasm are the **module** variant, bundled by Vite (`?url`), never fetched from a CDN: in a module worker the library's `importScripts` throws and it falls back to `import()`, which only the module build supports.
  - **Frames never touch the main thread**: a `MediaStreamTrackProcessor`'s stream of `VideoFrame`s is transferred to the worker; where there's none, `requestVideoFrameCallback` → `createImageBitmap` → transfer (`operatorFrames.ts`). The worker keeps only the latest frame and closes the rest, so a slow detection drops frames instead of falling behind.
  - **Each detection** goes through `createPoseFollower` and posts `{ follow, image, seenAt }`: the follow for the input source, the 33 image points for the overlay, and the wall-clock time the frame arrived. The source reads the latest follow each control tick; the first read of each result logs its age (the last 300, `operatorLatency()`: median and 90th percentile).
  - **The tile**: while it's open the strip shows one more tile (`OperatorTile`, key `operator`) labelled with the device's own name (no label when the device gives none): the live video mirrored like a selfie view, with shoulders, elbows, wrists and hips drawn over it in the accent, 2 px, redrawn only when new points arrive. Stopping the drive (Esc) stops every track and ends the worker.
  - **The estimator loads when the camera opens, and the drive starts once it's ready.** `joinOperatorCamera` resolves on the worker's `ready` or its first `error`; a load that fails on GPU and on CPU closes the camera and refuses with the same “Couldn’t open the camera.”, so the robot never sits still under a live tile with nothing saying why. Stopping while it loads closes the camera and starts nothing (`openSession` claims the session before it waits). It never loads with a stand-in feed waiting: its GPU set-up can hold the worker's thread for seconds on a loaded machine.
  - **From pose to robot** (`poseFollow.ts`): BlazePose's 33 world landmarks become `mocapBones` keypoints in a Y-up metre frame (`poseLandmarks.ts`: `[x, −y, −z]`, limb points under 0.5 visibility dropped), smoothed per coordinate by a One Euro filter (`oneEuro.ts`, min cutoff 1 Hz, β 3, chosen on the tests' noise and step). A humanoid goes through `createRetargeter` with `upperBody: true` (knees, ankles and toes dropped first, so a desk camera that can't see legs still calibrates on its first 10 still frames), and only the joints `poseJoints` names are sent. A fixed arm follows the right wrist's motion relative to the right shoulder since the rest frame, divided by the person's arm length and scaled to the arm's reach (`tipFollow.ts`). For a fixed arm, the left hand's pinch (`pinch`, thumb knuckle to index knuckle over the hand's size; Pose only sees knuckles) is the grip; the pose drive has no grip, so a humanoid ignores it. The rig reaches the worker without parts, with a table of its rest points (`pointTable.ts`) in place of the viewport's `pointOf`. `poseFollow.test.ts` runs `raiseRightArm()` (a synthesized 30 fps sequence: still, right arm raised forward, elbow bent to 90°, left hand pinching) over the G1-style `humanoid()`: the hand ends forward and up, the elbow lands within 10° of the person's on a rig whose elbow can bend 90° (the stock fixture's stops at 60°), no leg joint is sent, and the grip closes.
  - **Hand** (`handFollow.ts`, the `hand` input): the wrist's motion since the first hand seen, in metres. The hand's image points give its move across the picture, and the ratio of its palm's real size (world points, metres) to its size in the picture turns that into metres; a change in that size is depth. Axes follow the body's (`toHuman`: picture x is scene x, picture down is scene down, nothing mirrored). For a fixed arm the target is the arm's start plus that move × reach ÷ `FALLBACK_ARM_M` (0.55 m), so a 10 cm move is 18% of the reach. The grip is the thumb tip to index tip over the palm's size, 0.7 open to 0.25 closed, One Euro smoothed. While a hand is in the session its pinch is also the `camera` source's grip, in place of the pose model's knuckle pinch. A robot with joints gets nothing from it.
  - **Face** (`faceFollow.ts`, the `face` input): the face's centre (forehead, chin and cheeks) as an offset from the picture's centre, turned into a bearing with a nominal laptop lens (`laptopLens.ts`: 70° across, 4:3), and the head's yaw and pitch from the facial transformation matrix. The bearing becomes the pan and tilt targets, so a robot whose camera sat where the computer's does would centre the face; yaw and pitch are in the follow but drive nothing. `lookJoints` (`lookJoints.ts`) picks the joints: a pair named `pan` and `tilt` first, else the two revolute joints nearest a camera part walking toward the root, the one nearer the root being pan. Each carries a sign, found by turning the joint 10° and seeing whether the lens turns right or up, so "toward the face" holds whatever the axis. The `joints` `InputRobot` carries them as `look`.
  - **Latency**, from a frame reaching the worker to the drive reading its result (`__studio.operatorCamera().latency`): median 72–83 ms, 90th percentile 135–141 ms for the body, on an Apple M5 Pro running the production bundle in Chrome with the GPU delegate and a looped photo of a person as the camera, while other jobs held the load average near 45. The camera's own capture delay isn't in it, and it hasn't been measured inside the packaged app. Hand and face haven't been measured; the log counts the first read of each kind's result, so `latency` covers whichever run.
  - **In tests** `__studio.feedPose(samples)` stands in for the estimator: from then on the worker ignores the camera and runs the samples through a fresh follower at 30 fps, posting a thrown error as `error`, and `fed` when they run out (`__studio.operatorCamera().feeding` turns false once every feed has). Gestures read the samples' own times, carried on from the last feed, so a loaded machine that feeds slowly still sees a 2.5 Hz wave. Called before Drive with Camera, it's held until the camera opens and the worker never loads the estimator. `e2e/cameraDrive.e2e.test.ts` runs Chrome's fake camera (`FAKE_CAMERA` in `harness.ts`) and feeds `raiseRightArm()` to the G1. `__studio.feedHand` and `__studio.feedFace` do the same for a hand and a face. `handFollow.test.ts` runs `handMovesRight()` (21 image and world points at 30 fps: still, the wrist 10 cm to +x, then a pinch) on `arm-3dof`: the target moves 0.10 × reach ÷ 0.55 to the right, under 3 mm on the other axes, and the grip closes. `faceFollow.test.ts` runs `faceOffCentre()` (478 points, the centre at 0.74, 0.36) on `panTilt()`, a fixed pan-tilt with a camera built in code: the bearing is 18.6° right and 8.4° up, and under a `PoseDrive` the pan joint's error to it shrinks every step until it's under 1°. `e2e/inputs.e2e.test.ts` feeds both through the fake camera and checks the arm's hand moves right and the pan joint reaches its target.
  - **Gestures** (`src/cameras/gestures/`): the same worker turns each body detection into named gesture events, so a movement can drive a Case's logic as well as the robot's joints. Recognition sends only the event's name out of the worker, and a recording sends its trimmed example. No frame or keypoint is written anywhere; only a taught gesture's examples are saved, in its Case.
    - **The frame** (`gestureFrame.ts`): 13 points (nose, ears, elbows, wrists and the pose model's index, pinky and thumb points) in a body frame: origin between the shoulders, x from the right shoulder to the left, y up (world up made square to x), z forward, divided by the shoulder width. A child and an adult, or a person turned 20°, give the same numbers. A limb point under 0.5 visibility is `NaN`; the head points are kept, since the head is in view whenever the shoulders are. A frame without both shoulders clears the window.
    - **Built in** (`builtinGestures.ts`): pure functions over the last 1.5 s of frames, in shoulder widths. **Wave**: a wrist above the shoulders and its elbow for 80% of a window of at least 1 s, with three swings of 0.2 or more across the elbow. **Raise Hand**: one wrist above 1, its elbow above 0.25, the other hand down, still for 0.5 s. **Raise Both Hands**: both, for 0.4 s. **Point Left** and **Point Right** (the person's own left and right): an arm straight to 85% of its length and within 37° of sideways, for 0.5 s. **Thumbs Up**: the thumb point above the index and pinky points by 0.4 of the hand's size, the hand in front of the body below the shoulders. **Open Palm**: the index and pinky points above the wrist, the wrist above the elbow and 0.5 in front. **Clap**: the wrists within 0.35 of each other after being 0.8 apart in the 0.65 s before. **Nod**: two pitch swings of 10° or more with the yaw within 12°. **Shake Head**: three yaw swings of 15° or more with the pitch within 12°. Head angles and wave swings are smoothed over five frames first. The held ones need the wrist still within 0.2. Thumbs Up and Open Palm read the pose model's three hand points, which are knuckle estimates.
    - **Taught** (`gestureMatch.ts`): a gesture's examples are sequences of the 39 numbers at 15 fps, up to 45 frames (3 s), trimmed to where the body moved (0.08 from the first and last frames). Matching runs open-begin dynamic time warping in one column per example (`SpringMatch`), so each frame costs the example's length in point distances. The cost is the mean point distance along the path; a gesture fires below 1.8 times the largest cost between its own examples, and never below 0.12. On the fixtures a fourth salute costs 0.04 and the nearest other movement 0.19.
    - **Events** (`gestureRecognizer.ts`, `gestureTap.ts`): a gesture fires when it's first seen and again only after it has been gone for 0.5 s, so a wave held for 3 s fires once and two claps a second apart fire twice. Each posts `{ type: 'gesture', name }`; `operatorCamera.ts` emits it as the named event `gesture` with the name as its value (`emitInputEvent`, `src/sim/inputEvents.ts`), the same path as a phone press (§8h). `record(ms)` keeps the 15 fps frames until they span that long by their own clock, or until no frame has come for 1 s, and posts the trimmed example, or `null` when nothing moved. Both clocks start at the first frame after `record`, so a recording waits for a late feed or a stalled camera instead of ending empty; `gestures` sets the taught list. Teach Gesture's Record stays off for a new name once the Case holds `MAX_TAUGHT_GESTURES` (8; untaught names count), and `withGesture` won't add one past it, so a Case never stops parsing. `TeachGestureHost` closes the sheet when the camera closes, and sends the taught list only when the stored Case's `gestures` change: sending it again resets every match in progress, and the parsed Case is a new object on every render.
    - **Cost**: 0.13 ms a frame for every built-in and two taught gestures of three examples each, on an Apple M5 Pro under a load average near 50 (`gestures.test.ts`, which fails over 2 ms).
    - **Tests**: `fixtures/body.ts` poses a synthesized body (joint directions, three hand shapes, head pitch and yaw, size, noise and facing) and `fixtures/gestures.ts` moves it through every gesture, a salute and an arm circle in four variants, and someone walking past who stops to scratch their head. `gestures.test.ts` checks each built-in fires once on its own fixture, a 3 s wave once, a smaller person further away turned 20°, taught gestures from three examples, and nothing on the walk past. `src/cases/gestureLogic.test.ts` pairs each with a Case's logic. `e2e/inputs.e2e.test.ts` feeds a wave through `__studio.feedPose` and reads one `gesture` event from `__studio.inputEvents()`, then teaches a salute through ⌘K and fires it.
- **Silhouette sources** (`silhouetteSource.ts`, `silhouette.ts`): what Calibrate Servos (§8k) watches a robot through. A `SilhouetteSource` is `{ view, frame() }`: a camera view from the one camera model and a function that returns the robot's silhouette now, a 128 × 96 mask (`Silhouette`), or null. `calibrationView` picks the view: the first stationary camera, or the framed Scene view when there's none. `silhouetteDrawer` draws any pose through that view's lens (`lensPoint`) on the CPU, with no WebGL: each robot part as the filled hull of its projected shape (a box's corners, a cylinder's two rings, a sphere's rings), so the calibrator's worker and the source share one drawing. The only source today is `emulatedCamera`: it draws the scene robot at the emulated servos' true link angles (`link_rad` on each status joint, §8k Emulated horns), so no hardware is needed. The operator camera isn't a source: its frames would need the robot cut out from the room first.
- **Frames to code** (`cameraStream.ts`, `streamPace.ts`): a running Case's code panel (§8h) asks for `frames(camera, { width, fps })` and gets `ImageBitmap`s of one view. The bridge picks the size and rate (320 × 240 at 10 fps unless asked, at most 640 × 480 and 15 fps) and calls `CameraTiles.stream(camera, { width, height, fps }, onFrame)`, which returns a stop function or a refusal naming the cameras there. Only the Scene and stationary views and the robot's cameras stream: the computer's camera (`operator`) is never one, since it sees the person and a panel's code is hostile (Security, §0h).
  - **Rendered in the camera worker, transferred, never copied**: each stream (`stream` / `unstream` in `feedProtocol.ts`) has its own target canvas, filled from the same 640 × 480 GL canvas as the tiles, with the latest poses and behind the same GPU fence. `streamPace.ts` decides which streams are due: one frame per slot of 1/fps, a missed slot skipped rather than caught up, so a stream never beats its rate, and the worker keeps a timer while any stream is open, so a paused sim still yields frames. The main side only looks up the stream's callback by id and hands it the bitmap (`liveFeed.ts`); streams asked before the feed starts, or kept across a restart, re-open on the next feed.
  - **Main-thread cost**: handing a transferred 320 × 240 frame at 15 fps to its callback took 0.004–0.006 ms median and 0.03 ms at worst; the whole event loop, message delivery included, was busy 0.30–0.41 ms per frame. Measured in Node on an Apple M5 Pro (load average 11–16), with a worker thread posting a transferred 307 kB buffer through a `MessagePort` to `feedReceiver` 60 times over 4 s, timed with `performance.now()` and `eventLoopUtilization`. The worker's rendering isn't in it. In the app, `liveFeed.ts` adds up the time each stream frame's hand-off takes, the bridge's post to the frame included (`streamCost`, read by `__studio.cameraStreams()`), and `e2e/caseCode.e2e.test.ts` fails on CI if a 320 × 240 stream at 15 fps averages 2 ms or more.
- **The mat tracker** (`src/cameras/mat/`, real tries in §8h): follows a real robot's base on a printed Case mat from a camera, at the camera's rate.
  - **Opening:** `openMatTracking(owner, mat)` (`matTracking.ts`, a module store) asks for 1280 × 720 through the operator camera's `requestCameraStream` and passes the frames to `tags.worker.ts` through the same `frameSource`, so there's no second permission path. One try holds it at a time: `claimMatTracking()` returns an owner id (0 while another holds it), and opening, closing and `onMatSample` take that id, so a try in another scene tab can't close or read it. Try on Real Robot is disabled in every tab while one is held. Closing stops every track and ends the worker.
  - **The detector** is AprilTag 36h11 in our own Rust (`tools/apriltag/`, built to `public/apriltag/apriltag.wasm` by `npm run build:apriltag`, Security §0h). It thresholds per 4 × 4 tile, finds dark blobs on a 2× decimated image for frames 1000 px or wider, fits a quad to each blob's convex hull, refines each edge at full resolution and decodes within Hamming 2 in all four rotations. `corners[0]` is always the tag's own top-left. The codebook has one home, `tag36h11Codes.ts`, handed to the module at load. On synthetic renders the corners are within about 0.05 px RMS. The Rust follows §0 in spirit: no comments, small functions.
    - **Conventions:** pixel (i, j) covers [i, i + 1) × [j, j + 1), so its centre is (i + 0.5, j + 0.5); x runs right and y down. Blobs are outlined by pixel corners. A quad is ordered so its signed area is positive, dark inside; its homography maps the square (−1, −1), (1, −1), (1, 1), (−1, 1) onto it. Decoding samples the mean of each cell of the 8 × 8 black square, where cells −1 and 8 are the white margin. `tags_init` takes the codes as little-endian u64s and the bit layout as 36 x and 36 y cell bytes.
  - **Solving** (`matSolve.ts`, `matTracker.ts`): with at least two mat tags in view, `homography.ts` fits their floor corners to the image. The focal length comes from that homography (principal point at the centre, square pixels) and locks at the median of the first 5 estimates. The camera's pose follows from it, and each base tag corner's ray meets the plane at the tag's height, which gives the tag's centre and yaw in Case coordinates. A camera looking almost straight down gives no focal length; after 15 views the tracker assumes a 70° lens.
  - **Scale check:** each frame measures the mat tags through the floor homography. A frame whose corners miss by more than 6 mm (blur, a half-covered tag) gives no base and counts toward nothing. A frame whose tags are off by more than 1.5% gives no base, and 5 of them in a row refuse with “Print the mat at 100% scale.”; one that fits resets the count, so stray frames over a long run never throw the try away. Fit to page shrinks each sheet about its centre, where its tag sits, so tags shrink while their spacing doesn't: a 97% print measures 0.971.
  - **Speed:** detecting and solving take 1.8 ms per 1280 × 720 frame against a 30 fps camera's 33 ms (`matTracker.perf.test.ts`, run by `vitest.perf.config.ts`; Node on an Apple M5 Pro at a load average near 100). In the worker each frame is first drawn to an `OffscreenCanvas` and read back as gray. It hasn't been measured on CI.
  - **Frames stay in the worker** and are never written to disk; only `{ t, base, tags }` samples reach the page.
  - **In tests** `feedMatFrames(feed)` stands in for the camera. Called before opening, it's held, no camera opens, and the worker renders each pose itself with `renderMatFrame` (`matFrames.ts`). That's a CPU ray-caster of the mat's sheets, tags and base tag through `lens.ts`'s lens model, supersampled only on pixels that straddle an edge, with seeded noise; `printScale` shrinks each sheet about its centre. The unit tests use the same renderer, so none need a camera or WebGL.
  - **Limits:** the base tag is taken to be level at its scene height, so a rover that pitches 7° onto its caster puts its derived pivot about 10 mm off while the tag itself tracks within 1 mm. No lens distortion is modelled, so fisheye lenses don't track. The camera strip doesn't show the tracking camera.
- **The agent sees through them.** `simulate` takes `cameras` (up to 3 names, matched by label or key): each requested frame comes back as each camera's view (`cameraShots.ts`, 640 × 480 JPEG); with cameras and no frames it shows the last moment. Unknown names come back with the list of cameras.
- **The agent operates through them** (`agentOperate.ts`, the `operate` tool, `/operate`): each call starts drive mode on the live sim if needed (the same access as Drive: arms by inverse kinematics, rovers, legs through a walk skill), holds one action (forward, left, turn, lift or target) for 0.1–5 s, pauses, and returns the scene view and the robot's cameras plus whether it fell and where the end effector is. The robot stays where the step left it; `restart` respawns it. The agent looks at the views before every next step, the way a person at the controls would. The user watches the same sim on the canvas and in the strip.
- **The depth eye** (`src/sim/eyes.ts`): a policy can observe a grid of MuJoCo ray casts (`mj_multiRay`) through a camera part.
  - **Geometry is decided once, in TypeScript** (`buildEye`): the carrying body's name, the lens origin in that body's frame (m) and one unit direction per ray in that body's frame, row-major from the top left, `[0, 0, 0]` where a fisheye has no pixel. 2–32 columns, 2–24 rows (16 × 12 by default), range 0.2–10 m (3 m by default), up to 4 eyes. Forge and `train.py` only cast what it says, so the lens maths has one home.
  - **Value per ray:** distance ÷ range, and 1 for no ray, no hit or a hit past the range. The carrying body is excluded; geom group 3 is reserved for geoms eyes can't see. Kinematics run on the current state before casting, in every runtime.
  - **In the contract** the values follow the body's own observation (12 + 3 × joints), eye by eye (§8d). `PolicySpec.eyes` carries the geometry; `isPolicySpec` checks the normalizer's width against it.
  - **In Skills** (§8i) it's the observation term `{ kind: 'depth-eye', eye }`, chosen with `draft_skill`'s `eye` option. The geometry is part of the environment's hash, so moving the camera means training again.
- **LiDAR scans** (`src/sim/lidar.ts`, `scanner.ts`): every catalog LiDAR (group LiDAR) on a robot body scans in the live sim, in tries and in headless runs, with nothing to set up. It's the eye's planar-scan geometry: one ring of rays in the sensor's scan plane, cast with the same `castEye` (`mj_multiRay`, the carrying body excluded, geom group 3 unseen) and read the same way (distance ÷ range, 1 for no return).
  - **From the spec rows** (`lidarSpec`, one parser, a test per catalog LiDAR): range is the upper figure of the Range row (the first line when it lists several, `40 m @ 10%`); scan rate is the Scan rate or Rotation row's figure, and 10 Hz when the row gives only a span (clamped into it) or there's no row; rays per scan are Sample rate ÷ scan rate, else Point rate ÷ scan rate, else the Resolution row's field ÷ step (`0.25° over 270°`), else the cap; the field is the Resolution row's `over N°`, else the FOV row's first angle, else 360°. Rays are capped at 720 per scan (0.5° over a full turn) so a 3D sensor's point rate can't flood the worker, and at 4 LiDARs per model. So an RPLIDAR C1 casts 500 rays to 12 m ten times a second, an A1M8 hits the cap at 5.5 Hz, and a Hokuyo UST-10LX casts 720 over 270° at 40 Hz. 3D sensors (Mid-360, L2, OS1…) cast only their horizontal ring. The minimum range isn't modelled.
  - **Where it scans from** (`src/data/lidarScan.ts`): each catalog LiDAR's optical centre in its mesh frame, measured from the datasheet's height above the mounting face and the mesh's head (meshes that include the cable aren't centred on the head), and its 0° direction when that isn't the mesh's +Z. The ring lies in the part's own horizontal plane, ROS `LaserScan` order: ray 0 at −field ÷ 2, counter-clockwise seen from above (a full turn starts behind and steps 360° ÷ rays; a partial one spans edge to edge). A LiDAR the table lacks scans from its part's centre.
  - **Runtime:** `buildMjcf` puts the geometry in `SimModel.scans` (the XML doesn't change, so tries' model hashes don't either). `SimEngine.scanner` casts each LiDAR when its period comes due after a step, so in every runtime it scans at its own rate. The sim worker posts the newest scan with the next frame, once per scan: the values and the hit points in scene mm, both transferred. A LiDAR off the robot (scenery) doesn't scan.
  - **On the canvas** (`three/viewport/scanPoints.ts`): the hits are 3 px points in the accent on `stage.overlays`, so captures never show them, in one preallocated buffer updated once per scan (no object per point). They show while a simulation is loaded and clear when it stops. `scanPointCount` on the handle, read by the dev `simState().scanPoints`, counts them.
  - **The agent's `operate`** adds one line per LiDAR to each reply: its nearest return in metres, its bearing from the sensor's front, and how many rays returned (`scanSummary.ts`).
  - **Not for policies yet:** training doesn't take scans. A skill's eye is still a camera part (`buildEye` refuses anything else), and a scan's ray count is over `eyeProblems`' 32 columns; the contract, `train.py` and Forge would read one as an eye of `rows: 1` once a skill can ask for it.
- **Not done:** the operator camera as a silhouette source; pixel (RGB) policies; eyes on Forge's SIMD, Metal and WebGPU engines (they refuse with the reason, and the job steps on C MuJoCo); depth and range noise; the real robot's camera feed; stationary cameras in the dataset export; LiDARs on scenery; scans during a replay (the canvas keeps the last one).
